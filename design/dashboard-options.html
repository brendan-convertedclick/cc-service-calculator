Dashboard — six ways to restructure the layout

The current Operations overview is one long top-to-bottom scroll: a row of six identical metric cards, two near-identical project lists, then a margin table and an AI panel. It has no focal point and no hierarchy. Below is a faithful recreation of today's layout, then six restructured directions. Every option keeps the same data and functionality — the three scope counts still filter, projects still open, margin is still there — they just organise it differently. The mockups are interactive: filter, click projects, try each option's signature move.

  • No focus. Nothing says "do this first" — the 2 that need action look like the 5 that don't.
  • Mixed-semantic cards. 3 cards are clickable filters, 3 are passive KPIs, all look identical.
  • Duplicate lists. "Needs attention" and "Recent" reuse the same row, hard to tell apart.
  • Margin buried below the fold despite being a headline business signal.
  • Low density. A narrow stacked column on a wide monitor, big empty gutters.

Why it's better

It collapses the old flat top-to-bottom scroll into a single answer to "what do I do right now." The five attention items become a prioritized, overdue-first queue with inline Open/Snooze, so action lives where focus already is. The 14 healthy projects fold into one disclosure instead of a second identical list, and the buried margin table is promoted onto the first screen. The ribbon separates the three interactive status filters from the three passive KPIs that used to look identical.

Trade-offs

The triage framing optimises for the operator-on-shift; a partner who wants a calm at-a-glance health summary has to expand disclosures to see the full portfolio. Snooze is session-only state here (no persistence/undo timer), and the queue intentionally hides the 14 on-track rows by default, which can feel like information is missing until you learn the disclosure pattern.

Why it's better

Tile size is the hierarchy: the biggest tile is delivery health, the tall tiles are the two things you actually watch (what needs attention, where margin is bleeding), and the small squares are the at-a-glance counts. The two semantics that were conflated in the old card row are now visually distinct — interactive KPI/filter squares versus passive signal tiles — and everything lives above the fold with no long scroll.

Trade-offs

A fixed bento layout is less elastic than a stacked scroll: on very narrow widths it must reflow to fewer columns, and adding a brand-new signal means finding it a slot rather than appending a section. Dense tiles also cap how much detail each can show, so deep drill-downs still live one click away in the project view.

Why it's better

It answers two different questions in two purpose-built places instead of one flat scroll: the left column is the steady state (scope mix, the three KPIs, margin) you glance at once, while the right column is the chronological stream of what actually moved — so "how are we doing" and "what just happened" never compete for the same row. Margin is promoted out of the basement into the always-visible state column, and the two old near-identical flat lists (Attention + Recent) collapse into a single typed, time-ordered feed where colored rails make the urgent entries obvious.

Trade-offs

A synthesised activity feed implies an event log the product doesn't fully emit yet — entries here are derived from project state, so real wiring needs timestamped events. The fixed two-column split is comfortable on wide monitors but gets cramped below ~1000px and would need to stack. And a stream rewards recency: a quietly-rotting project with no recent events can sink down the feed, so the state column and tree pills remain the safety net for "nothing happened in 9 days".

Why it's better

One persistent KPI ribbon answers "how are we doing" at a glance, while a single segmented control means the body shows exactly one thing at a time — no endless scroll, no two near-identical lists competing for the eye. The default segment is Needs attention, so the screen opens on the only question that matters: what do I act on now. The ribbon's interactive scope counts are visually distinct (hover "filter" hint) from the passive KPIs, resolving the conflated-semantics problem, and clicking one deep-links straight into the relevant segment, pre-filtered. Margin is one click away as a first-class, full-width table rather than buried below the fold.

Trade-offs

Only one body panel is visible at a time, so cross-referencing (e.g. an attention item against its margin row) needs a tab switch rather than a glance — the cost of enforced single-focus. The segmented control is a learned affordance: a first-time user must discover that the body swaps. Density per panel is high but breadth is hidden, so power users who liked seeing everything at once on a wide monitor lose the "whole picture" scroll. The ribbon fixes six cells horizontally, which gets tight below ~1100px and would need to wrap or scroll on narrow screens.

Why it's better

The portfolio's shape is legible in one glance: the red and amber lanes on the left are short and demand action, the long teal lane reassures, and "recently delivered" gives a sense of momentum — replacing two near-identical flat lists with one spatial mental model. Collapsing a lane lets you mute the noise (e.g. fold "on track" to focus on what's burning), and pulling the margin table up beside the lanes means the headline business signal is never buried below the fold.

Trade-offs

A board trades vertical scanability for horizontal space — on a narrow screen the later lanes scroll off-canvas, and the fixed-height columns cap how many cards are visible before they scroll internally. Cards carry less detail than full rows (no inline scope badge text), and the icon-only rail removes the always-visible client tree, so client-centric navigation now lives behind the filter dropdown rather than in a persistent sidebar.

Why it's better

The scoreboard hero gives an instant portfolio verdict — the segmented bar and "21 active · 67% on track" headline make health legible before you read a single row, with the four supporting KPIs (including margin, surfaced from its buried table) tidied beside it. One unified, sortable, filterable table then replaces the two near-identical flat lists and the tree: attention, recent and on-track projects live in a single dense index you can re-rank by status, name, engagement or activity. Far less scroll, far more answer-per-pixel.

Trade-offs

Dropping the project tree removes the always-on client grouping and the per-client expand/collapse muscle memory; heavy multi-client users lose that spatial map (a Clients view in the rail compensates). The single table mixes "needs action" and "all good" rows, so urgency depends on the user actually filtering or sorting rather than it being pre-segmented. The margin signal shares a column with last-activity, so it reads as a secondary cell rather than its own RAG table.
